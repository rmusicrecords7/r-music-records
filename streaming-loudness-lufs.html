Streaming Loudness and LUFS: How Loud Should Your Track Be?

Why −14 LUFS is not a mandatory upload target, how normalization works and why dynamics matter more than loudness wars.

Many artists still approach mastering with one goal: make the track as loud as possible. That approach often misses the point. Streaming services may normalize playback loudness, so a louder master can simply be turned down while retaining the distortion and reduced dynamics created by excessive limiting.

What LUFS actually measures

LUFS (Loudness Units relative to Full Scale) is a way to measure perceived loudness. Integrated LUFS describes the average perceived loudness across the entire track. The closer the number is to zero, the louder the programme is. It is useful for comparing releases, but it does not describe punch, transient detail or tonal balance on its own.

Streaming targets are playback references, not upload rules

Spotify commonly uses a playback reference around −14 LUFS in its Normal setting. Apple Music's Sound Check is often associated with a reference around −16 LUFS. These figures describe playback normalization behaviour, not mandatory mastering specifications. Platform settings, playback modes and product policies can change.

Normalization does not repair a master. If your track is substantially louder than a platform's reference, the service may turn it down. You do not gain a lasting loudness advantage; you may simply end up with a quieter version of an over-compressed master. A quieter master may be turned up by some services or remain quieter, depending on the platform and playback setting.

What happens when you chase loudness

  • Less transient impact: heavy limiting can flatten drum attacks and make the mix feel smaller.
  • More audible distortion: dense limiting and clipping can make vocals, cymbals and upper mids harsh.
  • Listener fatigue: a consistently pinned waveform can feel tiring, even when its average level is competitive.
  • Lost contrast: verses, choruses and drops have less room to create an emotional lift.

A practical mastering workflow

  1. Choose a loudness range that suits the genre and arrangement rather than forcing every track to the same number.
  2. Use a loudness meter to understand the integrated level, but also monitor true peak and listen for distortion.
  3. Compare your master with relevant commercial references at matched playback loudness. Louder versions will otherwise seem deceptively better.
  4. Check the track on monitors, headphones, a phone speaker and in mono. Pay attention to vocal clarity, low-end definition and the impact of the chorus.
  5. Keep an unprocessed premaster and avoid making irreversible changes just to hit a target.

So, what LUFS should you aim for?

There is no single number that guarantees a professional result on every platform or in every genre. A dense electronic track, an intimate acoustic recording and a dynamic orchestral piece have different needs. Start with the musical intention, preserve the dynamics that make the arrangement work, and use platform references as a final compatibility check—not as the creative brief.

The takeaway: the best master is not the loudest file. It is the version that keeps its punch, clarity and emotional movement after playback normalization.
